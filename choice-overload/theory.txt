CHOICE OVERLOAD & DUAL-PROCESS THEORY
Theoretical Background
=========================================================

1. DUAL-PROCESS THEORY (Kahneman, 2011)
-----------------------------------------
Dual-Process Theory proposes that human cognition operates through two
fundamentally distinct systems:

SYSTEM 1 — Fast Thinking
  Automatic, fast, unconscious. Requires no deliberate effort.
  Operates via heuristics (mental shortcuts).
  Primary source of cognitive biases.
  Examples: recognising a face, feeling uneasy about a price that
  seems too high, grabbing the first product on the page.

SYSTEM 2 — Slow Thinking
  Deliberate, slow, conscious. Requires sustained effort and attention.
  Follows logical rules. Limited by working memory capacity.
  Can correct System 1 errors — but only when activated.
  Examples: comparing 5 products on 4 attributes, calculating value
  for money, reading the small print.

Key insight: System 2 is lazy by design. The brain prefers to delegate
to System 1 whenever possible. Any factor that increases cognitive load
(fatigue, time pressure, too many options) shifts the balance toward S1.


2. CHOICE OVERLOAD (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000)
---------------------------------------------
The landmark "jam study" demonstrated that:
  - A display of 24 jams attracted more attention than 6 jams
  - But consumers were 10x MORE LIKELY TO PURCHASE from the 6-jam display
  - More choice → more browsing, less buying

The dual-process mechanism:
  Large assortments → high cognitive load → S2 overwhelmed → S1 takes
  over (heuristics) or consumer gives up (abandonment)

The paradox: more options should theoretically help consumers find
their ideal product. But beyond a threshold, the cost of processing
options (System 2 effort) exceeds the benefit of having more choice.


3. NEED FOR COGNITION (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982)
------------------------------------------------
NFC is a stable individual trait measuring the tendency to engage in
and enjoy effortful thinking. High NFC individuals are more likely to:
  - Activate System 2 spontaneously
  - Resist cognitive load effects
  - Make higher-quality decisions under complexity

In this model: NFC ~ N(0.5, 0.2), truncated to [0, 1]
  NFC > 0.6 → Reflective consumer
  NFC 0.4–0.6 → Average consumer
  NFC < 0.4 → Intuitive consumer


4. WEBER-FECHNER LAW (applied to cognitive load)
-------------------------------------------------
The perception of stimulus intensity grows logarithmically, not linearly.
Applied here: the perceived difficulty of an assortment grows as:

    cognitive_load = log(n_products) / log(100)

Going from 6 to 12 products feels much harder than going from 42 to 48.
This is why the overload effect is non-linear — it hits hard in the
6→12→24 range and plateaus at very large assortments.


5. S1 HEURISTICS IN E-COMMERCE
--------------------------------
When System 1 takes over in online shopping, consumers rely on:

  CHEAPEST: minimise economic risk. Common in commodity categories
    where quality differences are hard to perceive (e.g., batteries,
    cables, generic groceries).

  TOP RATED: social proof heuristic. Delegate the decision to the
    crowd. Dominant when quality is uncertain and reviews are visible.

  FIRST SEEN: position heuristic. Choose whatever is most salient.
    Explains why Amazon's top position converts dramatically better
    than positions 5–10, regardless of product quality.

Key finding of this model: S1 heuristics produce products with
significantly lower ratings than S2 rational evaluation (3.5 vs 4.7
average rating). The quality gap widens with assortment size.


6. MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS
---------------------------
The model suggests three design interventions:

  1. ASSORTMENT REDUCTION: Keep default views to 12 products.
     Let consumers opt into larger catalogues. The sweet spot
     between discovery and overload appears around 12 items.

  2. SMART DEFAULTS: For intuitive consumers (high overload risk),
     pre-select a "best value" or "most popular" option. Act as an
     external System 2.

  3. PROGRESSIVE FILTERING: Guide consumers through attributes
     step-by-step rather than showing all options simultaneously.
     Reduces cognitive load without reducing actual choice.
